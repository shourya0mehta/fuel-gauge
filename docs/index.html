Turn any fixed camera into a measuring instrument

Fuel Gauge is an open-source Python pipeline for fire lookouts, phenology cameras and webcams. Give it a folder of photos and a location. It keeps years of frames aligned, works out where the camera points from the skyline alone, ties every pixel to the ground and hands back a daily vegetation record you can compare across seasons.

$ pip install "fuelgauge[geo,seg] @ git+https://github.com/shourya0mehta/fuel-gauge"

From a folder of photos to a calibrated record

Fixed cameras get bumped, re-aimed and replaced, their colours drift, and nobody wrote down which way they point. The pipeline fixes each of those in turn. Every stage is a command and a Python function, so you can run the whole thing or take the piece you need.

1

Keep years of photos on one view

A camera on a pole drifts a few pixels a month, gets knocked by wind and maintenance crews, and is sometimes re-aimed or moved to a new mast. Registration puts every usable photo back onto one shared view, so a pixel means the same patch of ground in 2004 and 2018.

Under the hood

  • Frame screening

    Brightness, Laplacian variance (sharpness), contrast and clipped-pixel checks drop night, glare, blank and blurred frames before any matching.

  • SIFT on CLAHE greyscale

    Contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalisation evens out light between seasons and times of day, then SIFT keypoints are matched with Lowe's ratio test at 0.75.

  • 4-degree-of-freedom similarity by RANSAC

    Shift, rotation and scale only. Fixed cameras pan, tilt and zoom a little; a full homography overfits to clouds and moving shadows.

  • Keyframe tracking

    Each photo is matched to the recent keyframes of its stretch, relocalised against older ones when that fails, and a new stretch starts after four frames in a row are lost.

  • Loop closure

    Stretches are joined by matching their clearest keyframes and keeping a maximum-inlier spanning tree, with at least 30 inliers per link.

  • Relocated and featureless cameras

    A camera moved to a new spot keeps those years as a second view with its own regions. Open grass close-ups with nothing to match are measured in place instead of dropped.

from fuelgauge import track
res = track.track(frames, track.Matcher())
views = track.group_views(res, times)   # loop closure
moves = track.sustained_moves(times, tx, ty)

2

Measure change you can compare across years

Raw camera colour is mostly weather, haze and sensor drift. The measure step keeps the clear days, finds the vegetation on its own and corrects colour against rock and soil in the same frame, then writes a daily series that only ever looks backward.

Under the hood

  • Haze scoring

    Sobel edge strength of each registered frame is correlated with the anchor frame. A day counts as clear at 80% or more of its trailing 90-day norm, so a re-aim does not read as months of fog.

  • Automatic regions

    Blocks the segmentation model calls vegetation and that swing strongly with the seasons (5th to 95th percentile of weekly medians) are measured. No hand-drawn masks.

  • Drift-tracking white balance

    Blocks that stay flat all year (rock, soil, road, distant ridgelines) set a von Kries correction of red and blue against green, smoothed over a trailing 61 days so it follows the sensor, not snow or wet soil.

  • Vegetation indices

    GRVI = (G − R)/(G + R) and GCC = G/(R + G + B) per block. Cameras that take near-infrared twins also give camera NDVI, with exposure correction after Petach et al. (2014).

  • Causal smoothing

    A weekly 90th percentile, then a 15-day mean, using only data up to each day, so the series is safe to use as a nowcast.

from fuelgauge.measure import measure
daily, regions, info = measure("out/mycam_blocks.npz", "out/mycam")
daily["index"].plot()   # joined across views

3

Solve where the camera points from its skyline

Most public cameras come with a location and nothing else. The ridgeline in the photo is a fingerprint of where the camera stands and which way it faces. Match it against the skyline a terrain model predicts and you get the pose without a survey or a single ground control point.

Under the hood

  • Sky segmentation

    SegFormer-B2 trained on ADE20K, run through ONNX Runtime with no GPU, labels the sky. The skyline is the first non-sky row in each column, snapped to the strongest nearby edge.

  • Rendered terrain panorama

    Copernicus 30 m elevation, marched outward along every bearing with earth curvature and atmospheric refraction (k = 0.13), gives the elevation angle of the true skyline in each direction.

  • Fisheye lens model

    Equidistant projection with one radial term, θ = (r/f)(1 + k₁(r/f)²). Fire cameras are strongly barrel-distorted; a pinhole model leaves degrees of error at the frame edges.

  • Robust pose fit

    Heading, tilt and roll by bounded Powell search on a trimmed loss that ignores the worst 20% of columns, so trees by the mast and low cloud do not pull the fit.

  • One lens for the whole network

    Cameras of the same model share a field of view and distortion term fitted jointly across all of them (101.15°, k₁ = 0.080 for the eight HPWREN cameras here).

fuelgauge calibrate rm-e frame.jpg --yaw 90 \
    --lat 33.4008 --lon -117.1905 --elev 483
# {"camera": {"yaw": 90.33, "pitch": -0.86, ...}}

4

Put every pixel on the map

With the pose solved, each pixel's line of sight can be followed out until it hits the terrain. Every block in the frame gets a ground distance, coordinates, slope, aspect and a land-cover class, so photo measurements can be joined with any map layer.

Under the hood

  • Back-projection

    The viewing ray of each block is intersected with the rendered panorama; the first terrain cell it reaches is where that block sits on the ground.

  • Terrain attributes

    Slope and aspect from elevation gradients at the ground point, and the ESA WorldCover 10 m class (tree, shrub, grass, built, bare, water).

  • Usable-ground mask

    Sky, the camera's own housing and blocks beyond the terrain model's reach are dropped before anything is measured.

from fuelgauge import terrain
pano = terrain.render_panorama(dem, lat, lon, z,
                               yaw - 85, yaw + 85)
cam, loss = terrain.fit_extrinsics(cam, pano, sky, yaw)
ground = terrain.backproject(cam, pano)

5

Know what a camera network can see, and where to add one

Give it a list of camera sites and it traces terrain line of sight from each, counts how many cameras see every patch of ground, tests whether a smoke column above any point is in view, and ranks the hilltops that would close the biggest gaps.

Under the hood

  • Radial viewsheds

    Rays every 0.1° out to 30 km, sampled at half the elevation cell size, with earth curvature and refraction. A sample is visible when its elevation angle beats every sample before it on the ray.

  • Smoke line of sight

    For any point, whether a column 100 or 300 m above the ground there would rise into a camera's view, the way a lookout spots a fire before its base is visible.

  • Greedy siting

    Candidates are the highest cell in every 6 km square. Each round picks the candidate that brings the most unseen target ground into view and counts each cell once, the standard greedy answer to maximum coverage.

fuelgauge viewshed cameras.csv coverage.tif
fuelgauge site cameras.csv --k 10 > new_sites.csv

All 1,309 ALERTCalifornia cameras against a 90 m terrain model. Dark green is wildland two or more cameras can see, light green one, tan none. Orange dots are 1,000-acre fires since 2020 that started where no camera could see a 300 m smoke column.

Scored against field data, including when it loses

The evaluate module scores any camera series the way you would want a paper reviewed: leave one year out, on top of a baseline that already knows each site's seasonal cycle, so a predictor only gets credit for knowing a year was unusual.

from fuelgauge import evaluate
# samples: site, date, year, lfmc and your predictor columns
samples = evaluate.add_season(samples)
scores, preds = evaluate.compare(samples, {
    "camera": ["cam_g", "cam_d"],
    "satellite": ["ndii_g", "ndii_d"]})

Get started

Python 3.10 or newer. The core install needs only NumPy, SciPy, pandas, OpenCV and Pillow; add geo for terrain and seg for the segmentation model, which downloads once (about 100 MB) on first use.

# install
pip install "fuelgauge[geo,seg] @ git+https://github.com/shourya0mehta/fuel-gauge"

# a folder of photos from one camera, dates in the file names or EXIF
fuelgauge run photos/ out/ridge

# solve the pose of a camera from one frame
fuelgauge calibrate ridge frame.jpg --lat 33.40 --lon -117.19 \
    --elev 483 --yaw 90   # rough heading

# what a set of cameras can see, and where to add more
fuelgauge viewshed cameras.csv coverage.tif
fuelgauge site cameras.csv --k 5
from glob import glob
from fuelgauge.archive import process
from fuelgauge.measure import measure
from fuelgauge.cli import photo_time

paths = sorted(glob("photos/*.jpg"))
times = [photo_time(p) for p in paths]
blocks = process(paths, times, "out/ridge")       # register
daily, regions, info = measure(blocks, "out/ridge") # measure

from fuelgauge import terrain, viewshed
dem = terrain.load_planetary("cop-dem-glo-90", "data", 33.4, -117.2, 40000)
picks = viewshed.site(dem, existing=[(33.40, -117.19)], k=5)

What you get back

  • _blocks.npz

    Mean colour of every 24-pixel block in every registered photo, with validity, haze score, stretch and view.

  • _qa.json

    Per-photo screening metrics, the transform that registered it and the views it was joined into.

  • _daily.csv

    Daily GRVI and GCC per view, raw and white-balanced, and one standardised index joined across views.

  • _regions.png

    The anchor frame with the measured vegetation and reference blocks marked, to check at a glance.

  • _lookup.npz

    From calibrate: distance, latitude and longitude for every pixel block.

  • coverage.tif

    From viewshed: how many cameras see each cell, as a GeoTIFF any GIS opens.
